Banks allocate their portfolios across different types of loans and securities, and changes in bank-level capital requirements affect multiple assets’ regulatory penalties simultaneously. Although a large literature finds that stronger capital requirements reduce bank lending, these bank-level studies generally estimate an aggregate lending elasticity that combines individual asset-level elasticities with portfolio substitution. Predicting the effects of policies that change regulatory penalties for specific types of assets, rather than for entire banks, requires separating these quantities. I develop a new structural framework and empirical method to separate asset-level supply elasticities from portfolio substitution by using changes in bank-level requirements that shift different assets’ regulatory penalties simultaneously in the presence of a portfolio adding-up constraint. My method recovers a positive supply elasticity for every asset category and finds that banks’ securities holdings respond more to regulatory costs than loans do. Its predictions match out-of-sample bank portfolio responses to changes in capital regulation. Applying these elasticities to post-crisis capital requirements, I ask whether stronger capital regulation explains banks’ portfolio shift away from lending since the financial crisis. I show that capital requirements account for a relatively small share of large banks’ shift from lending to securities since 2011.
