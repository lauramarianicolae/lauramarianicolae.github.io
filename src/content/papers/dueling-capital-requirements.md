Banks allocate their portfolios across different types of loans and securities. A large literature finds that stronger capital requirements reduce bank lending, but these studies generally estimate the total bank-level response to a regulation that shifts multiple assets’ regulatory penalties simultaneously in a predetermined pattern. Evaluating counterfactual policies that impose a different pattern of regulatory penalties across assets from those observed in the data requires modeling how an asset’s supply responds to its own regulatory cost and to the costs of other assets. I develop a structural framework and empirical method to estimate asset-level supply elasticities separately from portfolio substitution. For identification, I use changes in bank-level capital requirements that alter different assets’ regulatory costs by different amounts, while the portfolio adding-up constraint links each asset’s supply to the costs of all other assets. I recover positive supply elasticities for every asset category, with securities holdings responding more to regulatory costs than loans do. Applying these elasticities to post-crisis capital requirements, I ask whether stronger capital regulation explains large banks’ shift from lending to securities since the financial crisis. I find that capital regulation accounts for about 28% of the aggregate shift toward securities and 35% of the cross-sectional convergence in large banks’ average asset risk weights since 2011.
