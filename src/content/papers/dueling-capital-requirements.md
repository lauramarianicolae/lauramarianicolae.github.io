Banks allocate their portfolios across different types of loans and securities. Many studies of bank capital regulation estimate how bank lending responds when regulation changes required equity for loans and other assets simultaneously. Evaluating counterfactual policies that impose a different pattern of regulatory penalties across assets than those observed in the data, such as changing an asset's risk weight, requires estimating an asset’s supply response to its own regulatory cost, holding other assets’ costs fixed. I develop a portfolio-allocation model and empirical method to estimate asset-level supply elasticities using changes in bank-level capital requirements that alter the equity requirements of different assets by different amounts. The model uses the fact that portfolio shares sum to one to separate each asset’s response to its own regulatory cost from portfolio substitution. I recover positive supply elasticities for every asset category, with securities holdings responding more to regulatory costs than loans do. Applying these elasticities to post-crisis requirements, I ask whether capital regulation explains large banks’ shift from lending to securities. Capital regulation accounts for about 28% of the aggregate shift toward securities and 35% of the cross-sectional convergence in large banks’ average asset risk weights since 2011.
