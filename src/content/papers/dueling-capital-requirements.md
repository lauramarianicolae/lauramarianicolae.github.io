A large literature finds that bank capital requirements reduce lending because equity funding is costly for banks. But capital rules usually change equity requirements on several bank assets at once, so the decline in lending reflects both the direct effect of higher loan funding costs and the substitution effect of changes in other assets’ funding costs. For example, a 1-percentage-point increase in a bank’s risk-based capital requirement raises required equity on risk-weight-1 loans by 1 percentage point and on risk-weight-0.2 securities by 0.2 percentage points, encouraging banks to substitute from loans to securities even though both assets’ funding costs have risen. To predict the effects of policies not observed in the data, such as raising equity requirements on loans alone, regulators must know each asset’s supply response to its own funding cost, holding other assets’ costs fixed. I develop a structural framework and empirical method to estimate each asset’s own-cost elasticity of supply using variation in required equity across banks holding the same asset and across assets held by the same bank. These elasticities govern each asset’s direct response to its own funding cost and its substitution response to changes in other assets’ costs. I recover positive supply elasticities for every asset category, with securities more elastic than loans. I use these elasticities to estimate whether stronger bank capital regulation explains large banks’ reduction in lending since the financial crisis. Capital regulation accounts for about 28 percent of large banks’ aggregate shift from lending toward securities.
