A large literature finds that bank capital requirements reduce lending, but these studies generally estimate lending responses to regulations that change required equity for multiple bank assets simultaneously. For example, a 1-percentage-point increase in a bank’s risk-based capital requirement raises the bank’s required equity on risk-weight-1 loans by 1 percentage point and on risk-weight-0.2 securities by 0.2 percentage points. Evaluating counterfactual policies that impose a new pattern of equity requirements across assets, such as raising a single asset’s risk weight, requires estimating an asset’s own-cost elasticity of supply while holding other assets’ costs fixed. I develop a portfolio-allocation model and empirical method to estimate each asset’s own-cost supply elasticity using changes in bank-level capital requirements that affect multiple assets at once. The model uses the portfolio adding-up identity to express each asset’s own-cost response and portfolio substitution in terms of the same elasticities. I recover positive supply elasticities for every asset category, with securities more elastic than loans. Applying these elasticities to post-crisis requirements, I ask whether stronger capital regulation explains the reduction in large banks’ lending since the financial crisis. Capital regulation accounts for about 28 percent of large banks’ aggregate shift from lending toward securities and 35 percent of the cross-sectional convergence in large banks’ average asset risk weights since 2011.
