Banks allocate their portfolios across different types of loans and securities. A large empirical banking literature finds that stronger capital requirements reduce bank lending, but these bank-level analyses generally estimate an aggregate elasticity that combines asset-level supply elasticities with portfolio substitution. Separating these two quantities is necessary to analyze counterfactual policies that change individual assets’ regulatory penalties, as opposed to simply rescaling bank-level requirements. I provide a new method to separate asset supply elasticities from portfolio substitution, in a framework that allows assets to vary in supply elasticity and banks to have different comparative advantages in supplying different assets. My method recovers positive supply elasticities for every asset category and finds that banks’ securities holdings are more sensitive to regulatory costs than loans. Its predictions match out-of-sample bank portfolio responses to changes in capital regulation. Applying these elasticities to post-crisis capital requirements, I ask whether stronger capital regulation explains banks’ portfolio shift away from lending since the financial crisis. I find that capital requirements account for about 30% of large banks’ shift from lending to holding securities since 2012.
